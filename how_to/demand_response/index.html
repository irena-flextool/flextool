    Demand response

    Demand response is demand that changes with the price or the needs of the power system: it is cut when power is scarce, or moved to cheaper hours. FlexTool has no separate demand response entity. Instead, it is built from units, storage nodes and connections. The sections below go from a simple price-sensitive reduction to EV smart charging. They share one small model in demand_response.sqlite, with one scenario per section.

    The model (alternative base, scenario no_dr). It has 48 one-hour time steps: a Friday (t0001–t0024) and a Saturday (t0025–t0048). The demand node town is served by base_plant (700 MW, 40 €/MWh) and peak_plant (300 MW, 150 €/MWh). Both are units without inputs, and their cost is other_operational_cost on the unit__outputNode. Demand that the plants cannot serve goes unserved at the penalty_up of town, 3000 €/MWh. The demand (MW) is:

    Friday 00–05 06–07 08–16 17–18 19–20 21–23
    demand 680 800 950 1080 1130 780
    Saturday 00–07 08–16 17–20 21–23
    demand 550 820 980 650

    So the price of town (price_t in the node results) is 40 €/MWh below 700 MW and 150 €/MWh between 700 and 1000 MW. Above 1000 MW it is 3000 €/MWh. In no_dr, 80 MW is unserved at Friday 17–18 and 130 MW at 19–20 (Loss of load in the node balance, and the slack_up_t of town per time step).

    The scenarios dr_decrease_demand, dr_increase_demand and dr_shift_demand of examples.sqlite are smaller versions of the same ideas on the node west.

    How to model price-sensitive demand reduction

    (demand_response.sqlite, scenario: dr_price) base - dr_price

    Consumers who are willing to cut their consumption when the price exceeds a given level are modelled as a unit that feeds the demand node. Each MWh the unit "produces" is a MWh of demand that is not consumed. The unit dr_reduce has:

    • a unit__outputNode dr_reduce – town, and no input;
    • existing: 150 [MW], the largest reduction;
    • other_operational_cost on dr_reduce – town: 200 [€/MWh], the price at which the consumers start to cut.

    The model uses the reduction only when it is cheaper than the other ways to serve the demand, that is, when the price would otherwise be above 200 €/MWh. In dr_price, dr_reduce cuts 80 MW at Friday 17–18 and 130 MW at 19–20, exactly the demand that was unserved in no_dr. In all other hours the price is at most 150 €/MWh and dr_reduce stays at 0. The price at Friday 17–20 is now 200 €/MWh instead of 3000 €/MWh. The reduction is the unit__node result flow_t of dr_reduce – town.

    How to model a price-elastic demand (stepwise price blocks)

    (demand_response.sqlite, scenario: dr_blocks) base - dr_price - dr_blocks

    A demand curve, where the demand falls step by step as the price rises, is a set of reduction units with rising costs. Each unit is one block of the curve. The alternative dr_blocks builds on dr_price:

    • dr_reduce gets existing 50 [MW] and becomes the middle block (200 €/MWh);
    • dr_reduce_low: 50 MW at 100 €/MWh (a block below the cost of the peak plant);
    • dr_reduce_high: 50 MW at 500 €/MWh.

    Results:

    • dr_reduce_low cuts 50 MW in every hour with demand above 700 MW, where the price would otherwise be 150 €/MWh. The price there is still 150 €/MWh, set by peak_plant.
    • dr_reduce cuts 30 MW at Friday 17–18 and sets the price there to 200 €/MWh. It cuts its full 50 MW at 19–20.
    • dr_reduce_high cuts 30 MW at Friday 19–20 only (700 + 300 + 50 + 50 + 30 = 1130 MW). The price there is 500 €/MWh.

    No demand goes unserved. More blocks give a finer curve.

    How to make demand response available only part of the time

    (demand_response.sqlite, scenario: dr_window) base - dr_price - dr_window

    Many demand response programmes can be called only at given times, e.g. on weekday daytime when an office or a factory is operating. The unit availability time series sets this. The alternative dr_window builds on dr_price and gives dr_reduce an availability of 1 on Friday 08–18 (t0009–t0019) and 0 at every other hour.

    In dr_window, dr_reduce cuts 80 MW at Friday 17–18, as in dr_price. At 19–20 it is outside its window, so it cuts nothing, and 130 MW of demand goes unserved although the price is 3000 €/MWh. On Saturday it is not available at all.

    An availability between 0 and 1 gives the share of the capacity that can be called, e.g. 0.3 for a programme that can call 30 % of its 150 MW at night.

    How to model load shifting as a storage

    (demand_response.sqlite, scenario: dr_shift) base - dr_shift

    Some consumption can be moved to another time, e.g. heating, cooling or industrial batches. Moving it means consuming less at one time and more at another, and that is a storage: a storage node connected to the demand node. Energy that flows from the storage node to town is consumption cut now. Energy that flows from town to the storage node is that consumption made up at another time. The alternative dr_shift adds:

    • node town_shift: node_type storage, existing 500 [MWh] (how much consumption can be moved), storage_binding_method bind_within_solve (all the consumption that is moved is made up within the solve), and penalty_up / penalty_down 3000;
    • connection shift_link between town and town_shift: existing 150 [MW] (how much consumption can be moved in an hour), efficiency 1, transfer_method variable_cost_only and other_operational_cost 60 [€/MWh]. The cost applies in both directions and stands for the lost comfort or wear of moving consumption: 120 €/MWh for each MWh moved and made up.

    In dr_shift the storage delivers 80 MW at Friday 17–18 and 130 MW at 19–20 (420 MWh), and no demand goes unserved. The 420 MWh are consumed in hours with a price of 40 €/MWh: Friday 00–05 (20 MW, the room left on base_plant), Saturday 07:00 and Saturday 21–23. Some of it is made up on Saturday night. The storage cycles over the solve, so this energy also covers the start of Friday. Consumption is not moved out of the hours with a price of 150 €/MWh, since a 110 €/MWh price difference does not pay the 120 €/MWh of a round trip. With a lower other_operational_cost, consumption would also move from those hours to the 40 €/MWh hours. When several hours are equally cheap, the hours the solver picks are one choice of several equally good ones.

    Energy that is moved can also be lost (e.g. a building that cools down): give town_shift a self_discharge_loss. The storage does not require the moved consumption to be made up within the same day or within a given number of hours. That would need a storage balance every N hours, which is a possible future option.

    How to model EV smart charging

    (demand_response.sqlite, scenario: ev_smart_charging) base - ev_charging

    A fleet of electric vehicles is a storage that is connected to the grid only while the cars are plugged in. Its energy is used for driving whether the cars are plugged in or not. The alternative ev_charging adds:

    • node ev_battery: node_type storage, existing 300 [MWh] (the fleet's battery energy), storage_binding_method bind_within_solve, and inflow −40 [MW] at 07–08 and 16–17 each day (the driving, 160 MWh a day);
    • unit ev_charger from town (unit__inputNode) to ev_battery (unit__outputNode): existing 60 [MW], efficiency 0.9, and availability 1 at 00–06 and 18–23, 0 at 07–17 while the cars are away.

    The charger cannot charge while availability is 0, so the battery must hold the energy for the drives during the day. In ev_smart_charging the charger has no flow at 07–17. It charges 320 MWh into ev_battery over the two days, 355.6 MWh from town, and all of it in hours with a price of 40 €/MWh. It does not charge in the Friday evening, where demand is unserved. For vehicle-to-grid, add a second unit from ev_battery to town with the same availability.
